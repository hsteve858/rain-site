Detailed lesson plan · Day 3

Water Cycle in a Bag

Students observe evaporation and condensation happening in a sealed system, and connect what they see to the vocabulary of evaporation, condensation, precipitation, and collection.

SettingIn-person, pairs
Duration40 minutes
Follow-upSame-week observation

Materials

ItemQuantityNotes
Quart-size ziplock bag1 per studentFreezer bags hold up better than sandwich bags
Water¼ cup per bagTap water is fine
Blue food coloring1 drop per bagOptional — makes the "ocean" layer visible
Permanent marker1 per pairFor drawing the water cycle diagram directly on the bag
Clear packing tape1 roll per 4 studentsFor securing bags to the window
Sunny window1 per classSouth-facing works best for visible results within a few hours

Timing

  • 5 minHook: revisit the KWL chart from Day 1 — what do we still want to know?
  • 5 minIntroduce the lab: model the steps once at the front of the room.
  • 15 minStudents build their bags in pairs, draw the water cycle diagram on the bag, tape to window.
  • 5 minPredict: students write a hypothesis about what they'll see by tomorrow.
  • 10 minWhole-class debrief: connect the activity back to the vocabulary (evaporation, condensation).

Step-by-step instructions

  1. Draw the water cycle. Using the permanent marker, have students draw a simple water cycle on one side of the bag — a sun in one corner, a wavy line near the bottom for "ocean," arrows going up labeled evaporation, clouds at the top labeled condensation, and arrows coming back down labeled precipitation.
  2. Fill the bag. Add ¼ cup of water and one drop of blue food coloring at the bottom of the bag, below the "ocean" line drawn in step 1.
  3. Seal and tape. Push most of the air out, seal the bag, and tape it to a sunny window with the "ocean" line near the bottom.
  4. Predict. Before leaving the activity, students write one sentence predicting what they think will happen over the next 24–48 hours.
  5. Observe over the next 1–2 days. Water will evaporate, rise, and condense as droplets near the top of the bag, then "precipitate" back down — visible without opening the bag.

Common misconceptions to watch for

  • Students often think water "disappears" during evaporation rather than changing state and remaining in the system — revisit this explicitly during the debrief.
  • Students may confuse condensation with precipitation; both involve visible water, but condensation is the collecting of droplets, precipitation is when they fall.
  • Some students think the water cycle needs a living thing to "make" it happen — reinforce that it is driven by heat energy from the sun.

Formative check

At the end of the debrief, students complete a 2-question exit ticket: (1) label evaporation and condensation on a blank diagram, and (2) explain in one sentence what powers the water cycle. This feeds directly into the final model project on Day 9.
